# Jimmy Page: Course Content

This file is the source of truth for everything the app teaches. The app should hold all of this knowledge, so a person can learn from the app alone, with no outside help.

## Course goal (show at the top of the Learn tab)

By the end you'll know which notes fit each chord, and how to turn one small idea into four bars.

Who it is for: a guitarist who knows the A minor pentatonic box but mostly runs up and down it when trying to improvise.

## How every lesson is built

Each lesson has the same five parts, in this order:

1. Learn: a short, plain explanation (what it is and why it matters).
2. Hear it: an audio example. Always play the sound before naming the idea.
3. Try it: a small exercise in the app.
4. Check: a pass or "not yet" result, worked out by fixed rules (no AI).
5. Remember: one line to keep.

Each lesson also has a chip: Fact (true by definition, can be looked up) or Common guideline (a habit many players use; trust your ears).

## Notes available in the app

Three strings (G, B, high e), frets 5 to 8, plus the blue note added in Lesson 8.

| Note | Where | Key |
| --- | --- | --- |
| C4 | G string, fret 5 | C |
| D4 | G string, fret 7 | D |
| E♭4 (blue note, Lesson 8 onward) | G string, fret 8 | Shift+D |
| E4 | B string, fret 5 | E |
| G4 | B string, fret 8 | G |
| A4 | high e string, fret 5 | A |
| C5 | high e string, fret 8 | Shift+C |

Backing: four bars of 4/4, chords A minor, F, G, A minor, one chord per bar.

## How examples are written

`E4@0×2` means: play E4, starting at slot 0, lasting 2 slots. A bar has 8 slots (eighth notes: "1 and 2 and 3 and 4 and"). Slot 0 = beat 1, slot 2 = beat 2, slot 4 = beat 3, slot 6 = beat 4. Odd slots (1, 3, 5, 7) are off the beat. `rest@2×2` means silence.

## Chord notes reference

| Chord | All its notes | Chord notes you have in the box |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A minor | A, C, E | A4, C4, C5, E4 |
| F | F, A, C | A4, C4, C5 (F is not in your five notes) |
| G | G, B, D | G4, D4 (B is not in your five notes) |

## Lesson 1: Key, scale and chord

Chip: Fact

**Learn** Three words get mixed up, so here they are side by side.

Key: the home of the whole song. In A minor, everything feels like it wants to come back to A. The key stays the same.

Scale: the set of notes you play from. The A minor pentatonic is A, C, D, E, G. It also stays the same.

Chord: a few notes the backing plays together. It changes, usually every bar. Here: A minor, then F, then G, then A minor.

You play notes from the scale. The backing plays the chords. The key is the home they share.

**Hear it**

1. The A minor chord on its own for one bar.
2. Your five notes, one after another, over the A minor chord: `C4@0×1, D4@1×1, E4@2×1, G4@3×1, A4@4×1, C5@5×3`.
3. All four chords in a row (A minor, F, G, A minor), so the change from bar to bar is audible.

**Try it** Press Play. While the four chords loop, click or press any of your notes. Listen for the moment each new chord starts.

**Check** (three quick questions, multiple choice)

1. "The backing plays something new every bar. Is that the key, the scale or the chord?" Answer: the chord.
2. "The five notes you play from are the key, the scale or the chord?" Answer: the scale.
3. "In A minor, which note feels like home?" Options A, C, E, G. Answer: A.

Pass: all three right. Not yet: show the right answer with one line of explanation, then let them retry.

**Remember:** The key and the scale stay. The chord changes.

## Lesson 2: Each of your five notes has a job

Chip: Fact

**Learn** Every note in the scale has a number that says how far it is from home (A).

A = 1, the home note.
C = ♭3 (flat third), the note that makes it sound minor.
D = 4
E = 5, a strong, stable note.
G = ♭7 (flat seventh)

A surprise: these are the same five notes as the C major pentatonic (C, D, E, G, A). What decides whether they sound minor or major is which note feels like home, and that comes from the chords underneath. Over A minor chords, A is home.

**Hear it**

1. A, C and E played one at a time over A minor: `E4@0×2, A4@2×2, C5@4×4`. These are the 1, ♭3 and 5, which are the notes of the A minor chord.
2. The run `C4@0×1, D4@1×1, E4@2×1, G4@3×1, A4@4×4` over A minor. It ends on 1 and sounds like home.

**Try it** Turn on "Show numbers" so the fretboard shows 1, ♭3, 4, 5, ♭7 instead of note names. Play 1, ♭3, 5 (A, C, E).

**Check**

1. "Which note is 1?" Answer: A.
2. "Which note makes the scale sound minor?" Options C, D, E, G. Answer: C.

**Remember:** Same five notes, different jobs. A is home.

## Lesson 3: How chords are built

Chip: Fact

**Learn** A basic chord is three notes. To build one, start on a note of the A minor scale (A, B, C, D, E, F, G) and take every other note.

Start on A: A, C, E = A minor
Start on F: F, A, C = F
Start on G: G, B, D = G

Minor or major comes from the gap between the first two notes. On one string, count frets:

A to C is 3 frets. A 3-fret gap makes a minor chord.
F to A is 4 frets, and G to B is 4 frets. A 4-fret gap makes a major chord.

In the key of A minor, musicians number the chords by where they start in the scale: A minor is i (lowercase = minor), F is VI, G is VII (uppercase = major). The backing in this app is i, VI, VII, i.

**Hear it**

1. Each chord on its own for one bar: A minor, F, G.
2. Each chord's notes one at a time, using notes from your box:
   - A minor: `E4@0×2, A4@2×2, C5@4×4`
   - F: `C4@0×2, A4@2×2, C5@4×4`
   - G: `D4@0×4, G4@4×4`

**Try it** For each chord, click every note in your box that belongs to it, then press "Done".

**Check**

A minor: the selection must be exactly the notes A, C and E (any octave).
F: exactly A and C.
G: exactly G and D.

Pass: all three right. Not yet: highlight what was missed or extra.

**Remember:** A chord is every other note of the scale. F and G each have one note you don't have in your five.

## Lesson 4: Chord notes are the notes that fit right now

Chip: Fact (which notes are in which chord). Common guideline (that chord notes sound settled).

**Learn** Your five notes never change. What changes, bar by bar, is which of them are in the chord playing right now. Those are chord notes. They sound settled. The other notes of the scale are still safe to play, but they sound like they want to move.

Same note, different feeling: D is not in A minor (A, C, E), so over A minor it sounds unsettled. D is in G (G, B, D), so over G it sounds settled.

This is the main step from "running the scale" to soloing: aim for the chord, not just the scale.

**Hear it**

1. D held for a whole bar over A minor: `D4@0×8`.
2. The same D held for a whole bar over G: `D4@0×8`.

**Try it** Play the four-bar loop. The rings on the fretboard move to the chord notes each time the chord changes. End each bar on a ringed note.

**Check** In the user's four bars, the last note of each bar is a chord note for that bar's chord. Pass: 4 of 4. Not yet: list which bars missed, and the chord notes for each.

**Remember:** Aim for the chord, not just the scale.

## Lesson 5: Passing notes and landing

Chip: Common guideline

**Learn** Notes that aren't chord notes aren't wrong. They're passing notes: they create a little tension that a chord note then releases. D, for example, sits right next to C and E, so it naturally leads into one of them.

The last note decides how finished the phrase sounds:

Ending on A over the last A minor bar sounds finished.
Ending on C or E sounds restful but open.
Ending on D or G sounds unfinished, as if the phrase wants to go on.

**Hear it** Over the final A minor bar:

1. Ends on D (unfinished): `G4@0×1, E4@1×1, G4@2×2, D4@4×4`
2. Ends on A (finished): `G4@0×1, E4@1×1, G4@2×2, A4@4×4`

**Try it** Write bar 4 so it ends on A. Then change only the last note to D, and press Hear it to compare.

**Check**

Bar 4 ends on A.
Somewhere in the four bars, a non-chord note is followed straight away by a chord note next to it on the ladder C, D, E, G, A, C (for example D then E, or G then A over A minor).

**Remember:** Pass through tension, land on a chord note.

## Lesson 6: One idea, four bars

Chip: Common guideline

**Learn** Good solos often sound like speech: you say something, say it again, change it, then finish. You don't need many notes. Start with a short idea (three or four notes) and develop it:

Say it again: repeat the idea so people remember it.
Change the rhythm: same notes, new timing.
Answer it: turn the shape around, like a reply to a question, and land on a chord note. This is called call and response.
Finish: end on A.

Running up and down the scale sounds like an exercise. Repeating and changing one idea sounds like music.

**Hear it** A four-bar example built from one idea:

Bar 1, A minor (the idea): `E4@0×2, G4@2×2, A4@4×4`
Bar 2, F (said again): `E4@0×2, G4@2×2, A4@4×4` (A is a chord note of F, so the same idea still fits)
Bar 3, G (answered, shape turned around): `A4@0×2, G4@2×2, D4@4×4` (lands on D, a chord note of G)
Bar 4, A minor (finished): `G4@0×1, E4@1×1, G4@2×2, A4@4×4`

Then, for contrast, the same four bars as scale runs (C, D, E, G, A going up and down) so the user hears the difference.

**Try it** Go to Create, Round 1. Build four bars from your own idea using the moves.

**Check**

At least two bars share the same idea (same steps between notes, even if moved).
At least one bar changes the rhythm or answers the idea.
At most one scale run (three or more notes stepping the same way along C, D, E, G, A, C).

**Remember:** One idea, said again, changed, then finished.

## Lesson 7: Rhythm and space

Chip: Common guideline (the counting itself is Fact)

**Learn** Rhythm can make the same notes sound like a different idea.

A bar of 4/4 has four beats, and each beat splits into two halves: "1 and 2 and 3 and 4 and". That's the 8 slots in each bar.

A note that starts on an "and" is off the beat. Starting off the beat makes a phrase feel more alive.

A rest is a planned silence. Leaving space makes the notes around it stand out and gives the listener time to hear the idea.

A long note at the end of a phrase gives it weight.

**Hear it** Same three notes (E, G, A), three rhythms, over A minor:

1. Even: `E4@0×2, G4@2×2, A4@4×4`
2. Off the beat: `E4@1×2, G4@3×2, A4@5×3`
3. With a rest: `E4@0×1, rest@1×3, G4@4×1, A4@5×3`

**Try it** In Create, use a rhythm move or "Spread them out", and add at least one rest.

**Check**

At least one rest inside a bar (not just empty time at the end).
At least one note that starts off the beat (slot 1, 3, 5 or 7).

**Remember:** Where a note lands matters as much as which note it is.

## Lesson 8: The blue note

Chip: Common guideline (the note's name and position are Fact)

**Learn** Add one note to your five: E♭ (E flat). It sits between D and E. Its job number is ♭5 (flat fifth), and it gives that bluesy, tense sound heard in a lot of rock and blues.

It's very tense, so treat it as a stepping stone: slide through it from D to E, or E to D. Don't stop on it.

Where it is: G string, fret 8. Key: Shift+D.

**Hear it** Over A minor:

1. Without the blue note: `D4@0×2, E4@2×6`
2. With the blue note passing through: `D4@0×1, E♭4@1×1, E4@2×6`

**Try it** In bar 2 or 3, add E♭ once, between D and E.

**Check**

E♭ is used at least once.
Every E♭ is followed straight away by E or D.
E♭ is never the last note of a bar.

**Remember:** The blue note is a stepping stone, not a place to stop.

## Final challenge: your own four bars, no help

Chip: none

**Learn** No rings, no moves, no hints. Use everything from the lessons. The goals are shown before you start, so you know what you're aiming for.

**Goals** (show as a checklist before and after)

1. Every bar ends on a chord note for that bar's chord.
2. Bar 4 ends on A.
3. At least two bars share the same idea.
4. At least one rest inside a bar.
5. At most one scale run.

**Check** Score: goals met out of 5. Save the first attempt and show each later attempt beside it ("First try: 2 of 5. Now: 4 of 5.").

**Remember:** If you can do this without hints, you've learned it. Next, try it on your guitar over a backing track.

## What comes next (show as a roadmap page, not built)

These are real next steps, but they need the full neck, a real guitar, or more time:

- The other four positions of the pentatonic, so you're not stuck in one box.
- The major pentatonic and how it sounds over major chords.
- Modes as colors, starting with Dorian (adds F♯ over A minor) for a funky, jazzy sound.
- Seventh chords and how their extra note changes what fits.
- The CAGED system: linking chord shapes and scales across the whole neck.
- A listening mode that hears your real guitar and checks what you play.

## Review note

The lessons marked "Common guideline" are habits many players use, not rules. Trust your ears. This content should be reviewed by an experienced guitarist before being shown as final.
