# Guitar Theory You Can Play: Course Content (v3)

This file replaces THEORY_COURSE.md and COURSE.md as the source of truth. Do not invent content. Build every lesson from this file.

**Who it's for:** someone who can play a few songs on guitar but knows almost no theory.
**Course goal:** understand how the notes, scales and chords you already play are built, then use that to improvise your own phrases.

---

# Part 1: How every lesson works

Inspired by how practical guitar courses teach (sound and playing first, names second, tests last):

1. **Why it matters** (1 to 2 sentences that connect to the guitar the learner already plays)
2. **Teach**: short steps, one per screen. Each step has a few sentences and almost always something to **play on the phone guitar**. Target notes glow on the phone and on the laptop's phone frame. The learner plays them and hears them.
3. **Examples**: one or two worked examples, played or listened to.
4. **Practice**: the learner plays tasks **without** glowing targets. A "Show me" button lights the answer if they're stuck.
5. **Quiz**: 3 questions, never shown before the teaching. A mix of multiple choice and **"play the answer"** questions. Playing the right note is stronger evidence than picking an option. A wrong answer gets the short fix written for it, then a retry.
6. **Remember**: one line.

At the top of every lesson: a small link "Already know this? Skip to the quiz."

Each lesson has a chip: **Fact** or **Common guideline** (a habit many players use; trust your ears).

## Notation used in this file

- Strings: `E` = low E (thickest), `A`, `D`, `G`, `B`, `e` = high e (thinnest).
- `A:3 (C)` = A string, fret 3, which is the note C. Fret 0 = open string. All positions are within frets 0 to 12.
- **Play:** the listed positions glow, in order. The learner plays them. Pass when each is played (in order unless it says "any order").
- **Listen:** the app plays it (single notes or a strummed chord using the voicing table).
- **Find:** a practice task with no glow. Pass when the listed positions are played. "accept note X" means any position with that note name counts.
- **Rhythm:** a timing task. The click plays on the phone and timing is judged on the phone's own clock (so network delay doesn't matter).
- Quiz `(choose)` = multiple choice. `(play)` = the learner answers by playing.

# Module 1: Notes on the guitar

## 1.1 The 12 notes

Chip: Fact
**Why it matters:** Every chord and scale you'll ever play is built from just 12 notes. Learn them, and the fretboard stops being a mystery.

### Teach
1. Music uses seven letters: A B C D E F G. After G it starts again at A.
   - Play: A:0 (A), A:2 (B), A:3 (C), A:5 (D), A:7 (E), A:8 (F), A:10 (G), A:12 (A)
2. Those are only the letters. Between most of them is one more note. Walk up the A string one fret at a time and watch the names.
   - Play: A:0 (A), A:1 (A♯), A:2 (B), A:3 (C), A:4 (C♯), A:5 (D), A:6 (D♯), A:7 (E), A:8 (F), A:9 (F♯), A:10 (G), A:11 (G♯), A:12 (A)
3. You passed 12 different notes before A came back. Two pairs have nothing between them: B and C, and E and F. Play them and notice they're only one fret apart.
   - Play: A:2 (B), A:3 (C), A:7 (E), A:8 (F)

### Examples
- On the low E string, E is open and F is the very next fret: no note in between. Play: E:0 (E), E:1 (F)

### Practice
- Find: play every note without a ♯ on the A string, frets 0 to 12, in any order. → A:0 (A), A:2 (B), A:3 (C), A:5 (D), A:7 (E), A:8 (F), A:10 (G), A:12 (A)

### Quiz
Q1 (choose) How many different notes are there before the names repeat?
- 12 ✓
- 7 → fix: 7 is only the letters. Add the 5 in-between notes and you get 12.
- 8 → fix: 8 counts the starting note twice. The 12th fret note is the same note again, higher.

Q2 (play) On the low E string, play the note that comes right after E. → E:1 (F)
- If E:2 (F♯) → fix: There's no note between E and F, so F is just one fret up.

Q3 (choose) Which pair has no note in between?
- B and C ✓
- C and D → fix: C♯ sits between C and D. Only B–C and E–F are neighbors.
- F and G → fix: F♯ sits between F and G.

**Remember:** 12 notes: 7 letters plus 5 in-between notes. Nothing sits between B–C or E–F.

## 1.2 Half steps and whole steps

Chip: Fact
**Why it matters:** Every scale and chord is a pattern of two step sizes. On guitar they're simply 1 fret and 2 frets.

### Teach
1. A **half step** is the smallest step in music: one fret.
   - Play: A:3 (C), A:4 (C♯)
2. A **whole step** is two frets.
   - Play: A:3 (C), A:5 (D)
3. It works the same on every string.
   - Play: e:0 (E), e:1 (F), e:2 (F♯)

### Examples
- G to A is a whole step, because G♯ sits between them. Listen: G:0 (G), G:2 (A)
- B to C is a half step, because nothing sits between them. Listen: B:0 (B), B:1 (C)

### Practice
- Find: play any two notes a whole step apart on the D string. (Pass: same string, frets 2 apart.)
- Find: play any two notes a half step apart on the G string. (Pass: same string, frets 1 apart.)

### Quiz
Q1 (play) Start on A:5 (D). Play the note a whole step higher on the same string. → A:7 (E)
- If A:6 → fix: That's a half step. A whole step is two frets.

Q2 (choose) E to F is a...
- Half step ✓
- Whole step → fix: Nothing sits between E and F, so they're one fret apart.

Q3 (choose) On any string, a whole step is...
- 2 frets ✓
- 1 fret → fix: 1 fret is a half step.
- It depends on the string → fix: Frets are evenly spaced in half steps on every string.

**Remember:** 1 fret = half step. 2 frets = whole step.

## 1.3 Sharps and flats

Chip: Fact
**Why it matters:** Chord charts and songs use names like F♯ and B♭. They're just the in-between notes.

### Teach
1. ♯ (sharp) means one fret higher. C is A:3, so C♯ is one fret up.
   - Play: A:3 (C), A:4 (C♯)
2. ♭ (flat) means one fret lower. D is A:5, so D♭ is one fret down: the same fret as C♯.
   - Play: A:5 (D), A:4 (D♭)
3. So each in-between note has two names: C♯ = D♭, D♯ = E♭, F♯ = G♭, G♯ = A♭, A♯ = B♭. Which name is used depends on the key (Module 3).

### Examples
- On the low E string, fret 4 is G♯, also called A♭. Play: E:4 (G♯)

### Practice
- Find: play F♯ on the low E string. → E:2 (F♯)
- Find: play B♭ on the A string. → A:1 (B♭)

### Quiz
Q1 (choose) Another name for G♯ is...
- A♭ ✓
- G♭ → fix: G♭ is one fret below G. G♯ is one fret above G, which is the same fret as A♭.
- A♯ → fix: A♯ is one fret above A.

Q2 (play) Play E♭ on the D string. → D:1 (E♭)
- If D:2 → fix: That's E. E♭ is one fret lower.

Q3 (choose) One fret above E is...
- F ✓
- F♯ → fix: There's no note between E and F, so one fret up from E is F.

**Remember:** ♯ = up one fret. ♭ = down one fret. One sound can have two names.

## 1.4 Open strings and finding any note

Chip: Fact
**Why it matters:** If you can name the open strings and count frets, you can find any note on the guitar.

### Teach
1. From thickest to thinnest, the open strings are **E A D G B E**. A way to remember: "Eddie Ate Dynamite, Good Bye Eddie."
   - Play: E:0 (E), A:0 (A), D:0 (D), G:0 (G), B:0 (B), e:0 (E)
2. To find a note, start from the open string and count up one half step per fret. C on the A string: A, A♯, B, C.
   - Play: A:0 (A), A:1 (A♯), A:2 (B), A:3 (C)
3. Shortcut: fret 5 on a string is the same note as the next thinner string open. The one exception is the G string, where it's fret 4. Guitarists use this to tune.
   - Play: E:5 (A), A:0 (A), G:4 (B), B:0 (B)

### Practice
- Find: G on the low E string. → E:3 (G)
- Find: A on the G string. → G:2 (A)
- Find: D on the B string. → B:3 (D)

### Quiz
Q1 (play) Play C on the B string. → B:1 (C)
- If B:2 → fix: From B, one fret up is C, because there's nothing between B and C.

Q2 (play) Play A on the low E string. → E:5 (A)
- Wrong → fix: Count from E: F (1), F♯ (2), G (3), G♯ (4), A (5).

Q3 (choose) The open strings from thickest to thinnest are...
- E A D G B E ✓
- E B G D A E → fix: That's thinnest to thickest.

**Remember:** E A D G B E, then count frets up from the open string.

## 1.5 Octaves

Chip: Fact
**Why it matters:** The same note appears all over the neck. Octaves let you find it fast.

### Teach
1. Play the A string open, then fret 12. Same name, higher sound. That's an **octave**: 12 half steps.
   - Play: A:0 (A), A:12 (A)
2. Octaves also sit across strings. From a note on the low E or A string, go two strings thinner and two frets up.
   - Play: E:5 (A), D:7 (A)
3. The same shape from the A string:
   - Play: A:3 (C), G:5 (C)

### Practice
- Find: play E:3 (G), then the G an octave higher using the same shape. → D:5 (G)

### Quiz
Q1 (choose) An octave is how many half steps?
- 12 ✓
- 8 → fix: 8 counts letters plus the repeat. In half steps it's 12.

Q2 (play) Play the low E string open, then the E an octave higher on the same string. → E:0 (E), E:12 (E)

Q3 (play) Play the octave of A:5 (D) using the two-strings-over shape. → G:7 (D)
- Wrong → fix: Two strings thinner (G string), two frets up (fret 7).

**Remember:** 12 frets up = same note, an octave higher.

## 1.6 Reading chord diagrams and TAB

Chip: Fact
**Why it matters:** Most songs online are written as chord diagrams and TAB. Your phone guitar uses the same layout.

### Teach
1. A **chord diagram** shows the neck standing up: strings from thick (left) to thin (right), frets as rows. A dot is a finger, **O** means play the string open, **X** means don't play it. Here's E minor (022000). Play it one string at a time.
   - Play: E:0 (E), A:2 (B), D:2 (E), G:0 (G), B:0 (B), e:0 (E)
   - Listen: Em strummed
2. **TAB** uses six lines, one per string, with the **thin high e on top**. A number is the fret to play.
   ```
   e|-----------|
   B|-----------|
   G|-----------|
   D|-----------|
   A|-----------|
   E|--0--3--5--|
   ```
   - Play: E:0 (E), E:3 (G), E:5 (A)

### Practice
- Find: play this TAB, left to right. → A:3 (C), D:2 (E), G:0 (G)
  ```
  G|--------0--|
  D|-----2-----|
  A|--3--------|
  ```
  Then show: "You just played the C chord one note at a time." Listen: C strummed.

### Quiz
Q1 (choose) In a chord diagram, X means...
- Don't play that string ✓
- Play it open → fix: O means open. X means skip it.

Q2 (play) Play this TAB: `B|--1--` → B:1 (C)

Q3 (choose) In TAB, the top line is...
- The thin high e string ✓
- The thick low E string → fix: TAB is upside down compared with how you look at the guitar: the high e is on top.

**Remember:** Diagrams show fingers on the neck. TAB shows frets on each string, high e on top.

# Module 2: Intervals, the distance between notes

## 2.1 Counting intervals

Chip: Fact
**Why it matters:** Every chord is a stack of intervals. Counting them is how you'll understand chords.

### Teach
1. An **interval** is the distance between two notes, counted in half steps (frets). From C to E on the A string is 4 frets: 4 half steps.
   - Play: A:3 (C), A:7 (E)
2. Count the moves, not the notes: C→C♯ (1), D (2), D♯ (3), E (4).
3. A few distances have names you'll use a lot:
   | Half steps | Name |
   |---|---|
   | 3 | minor 3rd |
   | 4 | major 3rd |
   | 5 | perfect 4th |
   | 7 | perfect 5th |
   | 12 | octave |

### Examples
- A to C is 3 half steps (a minor 3rd). Play: A:0 (A), A:3 (C)

### Practice
- Find: play E:0 (E), then the note 5 half steps higher on the same string. → E:5 (A)
- Find: play D:0 (D), then the note 7 half steps higher on the same string. → D:7 (A)

### Quiz
Q1 (choose) How many half steps from G up to D?
- 7 ✓
- 4 → fix: Count every fret: G♯, A, A♯, B, C, C♯, D. That's 7.

Q2 (play) From A:2 (B), play the note 3 half steps higher on the A string. → A:5 (D)

Q3 (choose) C up to E is...
- 4 half steps ✓
- 5 → fix: Don't count the C you start on. Count the moves: 4.

**Remember:** An interval is the distance between two notes, in half steps.

## 2.2 Major and minor 3rds: bright and dark

Chip: Fact (the counts). Common guideline (bright versus dark).
**Why it matters:** This one half step is the whole difference between happy and sad sounding chords.

### Teach
1. Play A, then C. That's 3 half steps: a **minor 3rd**.
   - Play: A:0 (A), A:3 (C)
2. Now play A, then C♯. That's 4 half steps: a **major 3rd**.
   - Play: A:0 (A), A:4 (C♯)
3. Play both pairs again. Which one sounds sadder? (choose: A to C / A to C♯) Most people hear the minor 3rd (A to C) as darker and the major 3rd as brighter. Any answer moves on, with that sentence shown.

### Examples
- C to E is a major 3rd (4). C to E♭ is a minor 3rd (3). Listen: A:3 (C), A:7 (E), then A:3 (C), A:6 (E♭)

### Practice
- Find: play a minor 3rd above E on the low E string. → E:3 (G)
- Find: play a major 3rd above E on the low E string. → E:4 (G♯)

### Quiz
Q1 (play) Play a major 3rd above A on the A string. → A:4 (C♯)
- If A:3 → fix: That's 3 half steps, a minor 3rd. A major 3rd is 4.

Q2 (choose) D to F is a...
- Minor 3rd ✓
- Major 3rd → fix: D♯, E, F is 3 half steps, so it's minor.

Q3 (play) Play a minor 3rd above D on the D string. → D:3 (F)

**Remember:** 3 half steps = minor 3rd (darker). 4 = major 3rd (brighter).

## 2.3 The 5th and power chords

Chip: Fact (the count). Common guideline (strong sound).
**Why it matters:** The 5th is in almost every chord, and on its own it's the rock power chord.

### Teach
1. 7 half steps is a **perfect 5th**. It sounds strong and open. Play A, then E, 7 frets apart.
   - Play: A:0 (A), A:7 (E)
2. On guitar, the 5th is easy to grab: one string thinner, two frets up. Play them together: that's a **power chord** (A5).
   - Play: E:5 (A), A:7 (E)
   - Listen: both together
3. Same shape, new root: G5.
   - Play: E:3 (G), A:5 (D)

### Practice
- Find: play a power chord with its root on A:3 (C). → A:3 (C), D:5 (G)

### Quiz
Q1 (choose) A perfect 5th is...
- 7 half steps ✓
- 5 half steps → fix: 5 is a perfect 4th. The 5th is 7.

Q2 (play) Play the 5th above the open low E string, using the power chord shape. → A:2 (B)

Q3 (choose) The 5th above E is...
- B ✓
- A → fix: A is 5 half steps above E. B is 7.

**Remember:** 7 half steps = perfect 5th. One string thinner, two frets up.

## 2.4 Tension and release

Chip: Common guideline
**Why it matters:** Good melodies and solos create a little tension, then release it. This is the feeling behind "landing" a phrase.

### Teach
1. Listen to a C chord, then B moving up to C. B is a half step below C and pulls into it.
   - Listen: C strummed, then B:0 (B), B:1 (C)
2. Now B moving down to A over the same chord. It doesn't feel as finished.
   - Listen: C strummed, then B:0 (B), G:2 (A)
3. Notes that aren't in the chord create tension. Moving to a chord note releases it. Over A minor (A, C, E), play D, then step down to C.
   - Listen: Am strummed
   - Play: G:7 (D), G:5 (C)

### Quiz
Q1 (choose) Over a C chord, which note pulls most strongly to C?
- B ✓
- G → fix: G is in the C chord, so it's already stable. B is one half step below C.

Q2 (play) Over A minor, play D, then land on a chord note right next to it. → G:7 (D), then accept note C or E
- Wrong → fix: Am is A, C, E. From D, step down to C or up to E.

Q3 (choose) "Release" means...
- Landing on a stable note after tension ✓
- Playing louder → fix: It's about which note you land on, not volume.

**Remember:** Tension wants to move. Release is landing on a chord note.

# Module 3: The major scale

## 3.1 The major scale pattern

Chip: Fact
**Why it matters:** The major scale is the ruler that chords, keys and numbers are measured with.

### Teach
1. A major scale is a pattern of steps: **Whole, Whole, Half, Whole, Whole, Whole, Half**. On one string you can see it. Play G major on the G string.
   - Play: G:0 (G), G:2 (A), G:4 (B), G:5 (C), G:7 (D), G:9 (E), G:11 (F♯), G:12 (G)
2. The two half steps were B to C (frets 4 to 5) and F♯ to G (frets 11 to 12).
   - Play: G:4 (B), G:5 (C), G:11 (F♯), G:12 (G)
3. C major uses only notes without sharps or flats, because its half steps land on E to F and B to C. Here it is in the open position.
   - Play: A:3 (C), D:0 (D), D:2 (E), D:3 (F), G:0 (G), G:2 (A), B:0 (B), B:1 (C)

### Practice
- Find: play A major on the A string, starting open, using W W H W W W H. → A:0 (A), A:2 (B), A:4 (C♯), A:5 (D), A:7 (E), A:9 (F♯), A:11 (G♯), A:12 (A)

### Quiz
Q1 (choose) The major scale pattern is...
- W W H W W W H ✓
- W H W W H W W → fix: That's the minor pattern (Module 6).

Q2 (play) On the low E string, starting open, play the 4th note of E major. → E:5 (A)
- Wrong → fix: E (0), F♯ (2), G♯ (4), A (5). W, W, then a half step.

Q3 (choose) The notes of C major are...
- C D E F G A B ✓
- C D E F♯ G A B → fix: Following the pattern from C, the half steps land on E–F and B–C, so there are no sharps.

**Remember:** Major scale = W W H W W W H.

## 3.2 Major scales from any note

Chip: Fact
**Why it matters:** This explains why songs in G have F♯, and why chord charts use the names they do.

### Teach
1. In G major (3.1), the 7th note was F♯, not F, because the pattern needs a whole step there. That's why the key of G has one sharp.
   - Play: G:9 (E), G:11 (F♯), G:12 (G)
2. D major has two sharps. Play it on the D string.
   - Play: D:0 (D), D:2 (E), D:4 (F♯), D:5 (G), D:7 (A), D:9 (B), D:11 (C♯), D:12 (D)
3. Each letter is used once in a scale. That's why F major has B♭ (not A♯): the letter A is already used.

### Practice
- Find: play the 7th note of A major on the A string. → A:11 (G♯)

### Quiz
Q1 (choose) The 7th note of G major is...
- F♯ ✓
- F → fix: E to the 7th note must be a whole step, which lands on F♯.

Q2 (choose) Which notes are sharp in D major?
- F♯ and C♯ ✓
- F♯ only → fix: Follow the pattern to the 7th note: C♯ as well.

Q3 (choose) The 4th note of F major is...
- B♭ ✓
- A♯ → fix: Same sound, but each letter is used once. A is already the 3rd, so it's B♭.

**Remember:** Any note plus W W H W W W H gives its major scale. Each letter once.

## 3.3 Scale numbers

Chip: Fact
**Why it matters:** Numbers let you describe chords and melodies in any key at once. "1, 3, 5" is a chord in every key.

### Teach
1. Number the notes of a major scale 1 to 7. In C major: C=1, D=2, E=3, F=4, G=5, A=6, B=7. Number 1 is home.
2. The numbers move with the key. In G major, 1, 3 and 5 are G, B and D.
   - Play: G:0 (G), G:4 (B), G:7 (D)
3. In C major, 1, 3 and 5 are C, E and G.
   - Play: A:3 (C), D:2 (E), G:0 (G)

### Practice
- Find: play 1, 3 and 5 of A major on the A string. → A:0 (A), A:4 (C♯), A:7 (E)

### Quiz
Q1 (choose) In G major, number 5 is...
- D ✓
- C → fix: G (1), A (2), B (3), C (4), D (5).

Q2 (play) Play 1, 3 and 5 of D major on the D string. → D:0 (D), D:4 (F♯), D:7 (A)

Q3 (choose) Which number is home?
- 1 ✓
- 7 → fix: 7 is the last note before home. Home is 1.

**Remember:** Numbers are positions in the scale. 1 is home.

# Module 4: Chords (how the chords you play are built)

## 4.1 What's inside a chord

Chip: Fact
**Why it matters:** You already play C, G and Am. Here's what's actually inside them.

### Teach
1. A chord is three or more notes played together. The basic chord, a **triad**, uses notes 1, 3 and 5 of a scale.
2. Play the open C chord one string at a time and watch the names.
   - Play: A:3 (C), D:2 (E), G:0 (G), B:1 (C), e:0 (E)
   - Listen: C strummed
3. Five strings, but only three names: C, E and G. That's 1, 3 and 5 of C major. The extra C and E are the same notes in a higher octave.

### Examples
- G chord: play it string by string. Names: G, B, D (1, 3, 5 of G major). Play: E:3 (G), A:2 (B), D:0 (D), G:0 (G), B:0 (B), e:3 (G)

### Practice
- Find: play the open Am chord one string at a time. → A:0 (A), D:2 (E), G:2 (A), B:1 (C), e:0 (E)
  Then ask (choose): which three note names make Am? A, C, E ✓ / A, B, E

### Quiz
Q1 (choose) The notes of a G major chord are...
- G, B, D ✓
- G, A, B → fix: A triad skips every other note: 1, 3, 5. G, B, D.

Q2 (choose) The open C chord uses how many different note names?
- 3 ✓
- 5 → fix: Five strings, but C and E repeat. Only C, E and G.

Q3 (play) Play the three notes of a C triad on the A, D and G strings, any order. → A:3 (C), D:2 (E), G:0 (G)

**Remember:** A triad is notes 1, 3 and 5. Chords repeat them across strings.

## 4.2 Major and minor: one finger apart

Chip: Fact (the notes). Common guideline (happy versus sad).
**Why it matters:** The difference between a happy and a sad chord is one note, and often one finger.

### Teach
1. Play E major one string at a time.
   - Play: E:0 (E), A:2 (B), D:2 (E), G:1 (G♯), B:0 (B), e:0 (E)
   - Listen: E strummed
2. Now lift your finger off the G string: G♯ becomes G. That's E minor.
   - Play: G:1 (G♯), G:0 (G)
   - Listen: E strummed, then Em strummed
3. That one note is the **3rd**. E to G♯ is 4 half steps (major 3rd), so E is major. E to G is 3 (minor 3rd), so Em is minor. The root and 5th stay the same.

### Examples
- A to Am: the B string moves from fret 2 (C♯) to fret 1 (C). Play: B:2 (C♯), B:1 (C). Listen: A, then Am
- D to Dm: the high e moves from fret 2 (F♯) to fret 1 (F). Play: e:2 (F♯), e:1 (F). Listen: D, then Dm

### Practice
- Find: play the note that turns A major into A minor. → B:1 (C)
- Find: play the note that turns D minor into D major. → e:2 (F♯)

### Quiz
Q1 (choose) Listen: A, then Am. Which sounds sadder?
- The second (Am) ✓
- The first → fix: Most people hear the minor chord as darker. Listen again to the B string note.

Q2 (choose) A, C, E is...
- Minor ✓
- Major → fix: A to C is 3 half steps, a minor 3rd.

Q3 (play) Play the 3rd of the E major chord, on the G string. → G:1 (G♯)

**Remember:** Major = major 3rd (4). Minor = minor 3rd (3). Only the 3rd changes.

---

## 4.3 The root: the note a chord is named after

Chip: Fact (the root). Common guideline (root as the lowest note).
**Why it matters:** Knowing the root tells you where a chord "lives" and why some strings are skipped.

### Teach
1. The **root** is the note a chord is named after. In open chords, the lowest string you play is usually the root.
   - Play: E:0 (E), A:0 (A), D:0 (D)
   - Listen: E, A, D strummed
2. That's why the low E string has an X in C and A: skipping it keeps the root at the bottom. The lowest note of C is the C at A:3.
   - Play: A:3 (C)

### Practice
- Find: play the root of the G chord. → E:3 (G)
- Find: play the root of Am. → A:0 (A)

### Quiz
Q1 (choose) The lowest note of the open D chord is...
- D ✓
- A → fix: The D chord starts on the open D string.

Q2 (play) Play the root of the C chord. → A:3 (C)

Q3 (choose) Why is the low E string skipped in the C chord?
- So the lowest note is the root, C ✓
- Because E isn't in a C chord → fix: E is in a C chord (it's the 3rd). It's skipped so C stays at the bottom.

**Remember:** The root names the chord and usually sits at the bottom.

---

## 4.4 The chords that belong to a key

Chip: Fact
**Why it matters:** This is why certain chords sound right together in a song.

### Teach
1. Build a triad on every note of C major, using only C major notes. You get C, Dm, Em, F, G, Am and B diminished. The pattern for every major key: **I ii iii IV V vi vii°** (uppercase = major, lowercase = minor).
2. Play the roots up the A string.
   - Play: A:3 (C), A:5 (D), A:7 (E), A:8 (F), A:10 (G), A:12 (A)
3. Now hear them as chords.
   - Listen: C, Dm, Em, F, G, Am strummed

### Practice
- Find: play the root of chord V in C major on the A string. → A:10 (G)
- Find: play the root of chord vi in C major. → accept note A

### Quiz
Q1 (choose) Which chord belongs to C major?
- D minor ✓
- D major → fix: D major needs F♯, which isn't in C major. D minor (D, F, A) fits.

Q2 (choose) In G major (G A B C D E F♯), chord vi is...
- E minor ✓
- E major → fix: vi is lowercase, so minor: E, G, B.

Q3 (play) Play the root of chord IV in C major on the A string. → A:8 (F)

**Remember:** Every major key: I ii iii IV V vi vii°.

# Module 5: Keys and chord progressions

## 5.1 What a key is

Chip: Fact
**Why it matters:** Knowing the key tells you which chords and notes will fit a song.

### Teach
1. A **key** is a scale plus the chords built from it, with one home. In C major, home is the note C and the C chord.
2. Listen: C, F, G, then back to C. It sounds finished.
   - Listen: C, F, G, C strummed
3. Now C, F, G and stop. It sounds like it's waiting to go home.
   - Listen: C, F, G strummed

### Practice
- Find: play the home note of G major on the low E string. → E:3 (G)

### Quiz
Q1 (choose) A song's key is...
- A scale and its chords, with one home ✓
- The first chord of the song → fix: Songs often start at home, but the key is about which chord feels like home.

Q2 (choose) In G major, the home chord is...
- G ✓
- D → fix: D is chord V. It pulls back toward G.

Q3 (choose) Which group belongs to G major?
- G, C, D, Em ✓
- G, C, D, E → fix: E major needs G♯, which isn't in G major.

**Remember:** A key is a scale plus its chords, with one home.

---

## 5.2 Chord numbers and common progressions

Chip: Fact (the numbers). Common guideline (where they're common).
**Why it matters:** Thousands of songs use the same few progressions. Numbers let you play them in any key.

### Teach
1. Chords are named by their position in the key. In G major, I–IV–V is G, C, D. Play the roots.
   - Play: E:3 (G), A:3 (C), A:5 (D)
   - Listen: G, C, D strummed
2. Same numbers in A major: A, D, E.
   - Play: A:0 (A), D:0 (D), E:0 (E)
3. I–V–vi–IV is in a huge number of pop songs. In C: C, G, Am, F.
   - Listen: C, G, Am, F strummed

### Practice
- Find: play the roots of I–V–vi–IV in C. → A:3 (C), E:3 (G), A:0 (A), E:1 (F)

### Quiz
Q1 (choose) "IV" means...
- The chord built on the 4th note of the key ✓
- The 4th chord you play → fix: It's about position in the key, not order in the song.

Q2 (choose) I–IV–V in A major is...
- A, D, E ✓
- A, B, C♯ → fix: Take notes 1, 4 and 5 of A major: A, D, E.

Q3 (play) Play the root of chord V in G major on the A string. → A:5 (D)

**Remember:** Numbers make progressions portable. I–IV–V and I–V–vi–IV are everywhere.

---

## 5.3 Finding the key of a song

Chip: Common guideline
**Why it matters:** Once you know a song's key, you know which scale to solo with.

### Teach
1. Use two clues. First: which chord feels like home? Usually the one the song ends on or keeps returning to.
   - Listen: G, Em, C, D, G strummed
2. Second: which key contains all the chords? G, C, D and Em are I, IV, V and vi of G major.

### Practice
- Listen: D, G, A, Bm, D strummed. Find: play the home note of this progression. → accept note D

### Quiz
Q1 (choose) A song uses G, C, D and Em and ends on G. Key?
- G major ✓
- C major → fix: The D chord needs F♯, which isn't in C major.

Q2 (choose) A song uses C, F, G and Am and ends on C. Key?
- C major ✓
- F major → fix: Every chord fits C major, and C is home.

Q3 (choose) A song uses D, G, A and Bm and ends on D. Key?
- D major ✓
- A major → fix: These are I, IV, V and vi of D major.

**Remember:** Find home by ear, then check which key holds every chord.

# Module 6: Minor keys

## 6.1 The relative minor

Chip: Fact
**Why it matters:** A minor and C major share every note. That's why the A minor pentatonic box also works in C major songs.

### Teach
1. Play the C major notes, but start and end on A. That's **A natural minor**.
   - Play: A:0 (A), A:2 (B), A:3 (C), A:5 (D), A:7 (E), A:8 (F), A:10 (G), A:12 (A)
2. Same notes as C major, different home. Over an Am chord, it sounds darker.
   - Listen: Am strummed, then the same notes
3. Every major key has a **relative minor**, starting on its 6th note. C major's 6th note is A.

### Practice
- Find: play the home note of G major's relative minor. → accept note E

### Quiz
Q1 (choose) Do C major and A minor use the same notes?
- Yes ✓
- No → fix: A minor is C major started from A. Same notes, different home.

Q2 (choose) The relative minor of G major is...
- E minor ✓
- A minor → fix: Take the 6th note of G major: E.

Q3 (choose) The relative minor of D major is...
- B minor ✓
- A minor → fix: The 6th note of D major is B.

**Remember:** Same notes, different home. The relative minor starts on the 6th note.

---

## 6.2 The natural minor pattern

Chip: Fact
**Why it matters:** The minor pattern explains why minor scales sound darker: the 3rd, 6th and 7th notes are lower.

### Teach
1. The natural minor pattern is **W H W W H W W**. You played it on the A string in 6.1.
2. Compare A major and A minor on the A string. Three notes drop a half step: the 3rd (C♯ to C), 6th (F♯ to F) and 7th (G♯ to G).
   - Play: A:4 (C♯), A:3 (C), A:9 (F♯), A:8 (F), A:11 (G♯), A:10 (G)

### Practice
- Find: play E natural minor on the low E string, starting open. → E:0 (E), E:2 (F♯), E:3 (G), E:5 (A), E:7 (B), E:8 (C), E:10 (D), E:12 (E)

### Quiz
Q1 (choose) The 3rd note of A natural minor is...
- C ✓
- C♯ → fix: C♯ is from A major. Minor has the lower 3rd, C.

Q2 (play) Play the 3rd note of D natural minor on the D string. → D:3 (F)
- If D:4 → fix: That's F♯, the major 3rd. Minor is one fret lower.

Q3 (choose) In natural minor, the step between notes 2 and 3 is...
- A half step ✓
- A whole step → fix: W H W W H W W: the second step is H.

**Remember:** Natural minor = W H W W H W W.

---

## 6.3 Chords in a minor key

Chip: Fact
**Why it matters:** Am, F and G is one of the most common rock progressions, and it's the backing you'll improvise over in Module 9.

### Teach
1. Build triads on A natural minor: Am, B diminished, C, Dm, Em, F, G. The pattern for every minor key: **i ii° III iv v VI VII**.
2. Am, F, G is **i, VI, VII**. Play the roots on the low E string.
   - Play: E:5 (A), E:1 (F), E:3 (G)
   - Listen: Am, F, G, Am strummed

### Practice
- Find: play the root of chord iv in A minor on the A string. → A:5 (D)

### Quiz
Q1 (choose) In A minor, chord i is...
- A minor ✓
- A major → fix: Lowercase i means minor. A minor's home chord is Am.

Q2 (choose) In A minor, chord VI is...
- F ✓
- Dm → fix: The 6th note of A minor is F, and VI is major.

Q3 (choose) Am, F, G is...
- i, VI, VII ✓
- i, IV, V → fix: F is the 6th note of A minor and G is the 7th.

**Remember:** Minor keys: i ii° III iv v VI VII. Am–F–G = i–VI–VII.

# Module 7: Rhythm

## 7.1 Beats and bars

Chip: Fact
**Why it matters:** Playing in time matters more than playing the right note. Every phrase lives inside a bar.

### Teach
1. The **beat** is the pulse you tap your foot to. In **4/4**, a **bar** has 4 beats. Beat 1 is the strongest.
   - Listen: click at 80 bpm, beat 1 louder, two bars
2. Tap along on the open A string, once per beat, for two bars.
   - Rhythm: A:0 (A), 8 taps, one per beat at 80 bpm (pass: each tap within 150 ms of the beat)

### Quiz
Q1 (choose) In 4/4, how many beats are in a bar?
- 4 ✓
- 8 → fix: "1 and 2 and 3 and 4 and" has 8 words but only 4 beats.

Q2 (choose) The top number in 4/4 tells you...
- How many beats are in a bar ✓
- How fast to play → fix: Speed is set by the tempo (bpm).

**Remember:** 4/4 = four beats per bar. Beat 1 is strongest.

---

## 7.2 Note lengths and rests

Chip: Fact
**Why it matters:** How long you hold a note, and when you stay silent, shapes how a phrase feels.

### Teach
1. In 4/4: a **whole note** lasts 4 beats, a **half note** 2, a **quarter note** 1, and an **eighth note** half a beat. A **rest** is a timed silence.
   - Listen: on D:7 (A): one whole note, two half notes, four quarter notes, eight eighth notes
2. Tap half notes: on beats 1 and 3 only, for two bars.
   - Rhythm: D:7 (A), 4 taps on beats 1 and 3 (pass: within 150 ms)

### Quiz
Q1 (choose) A half note lasts...
- 2 beats ✓
- Half a beat → fix: A half note is half of a 4-beat bar: 2 beats.

Q2 (choose) How many eighth notes fit in one bar of 4/4?
- 8 ✓
- 4 → fix: Two per beat, four beats: 8.

Q3 (choose) A bar has a half note and a quarter note. How many beats are left?
- 1 ✓
- 2 → fix: 2 + 1 = 3 beats used, so 1 is left.

**Remember:** Whole 4, half 2, quarter 1, eighth ½. Rests are timed silence.

---

## 7.3 Off the beat

Chip: Fact (where the off-beats are). Common guideline (the feel).
**Why it matters:** Starting notes between the beats is what makes riffs and solos feel alive.

### Teach
1. Count "1 and 2 and 3 and 4 and". Numbers are **on the beat**. "Ands" are **off the beat**. Stressing off-beats is **syncopation**.
   - Listen: two bars, strums on the numbers, then two bars with strums on the "ands"
2. Tap only on the "ands" for one bar.
   - Rhythm: B:5 (E), 4 taps halfway between the clicks (pass: within 120 ms)

### Quiz
Q1 (choose) Which is off the beat?
- The "and" after 2 ✓
- Beat 3 → fix: Numbers are on the beat. Only the "ands" are off.

Q2 (choose) A note starts on the "and" of 4 and rings into the next bar. That's...
- Syncopation ✓
- A mistake → fix: Pushing a note across the strong beat is a common, deliberate feel.

**Remember:** Numbers are on the beat. "Ands" are off the beat.

---

# Module 8: The pentatonic scale

## 8.1 From seven notes to five

Chip: Fact
**Why it matters:** The pentatonic is the most-used soloing scale in rock and blues. Now you'll know why it works.

### Teach
1. Start from A natural minor (A B C D E F G) and remove notes 2 and ♭6 (B and F). You get **A C D E G**: the A minor pentatonic.
   - Play: A:0 (A), A:3 (C), A:5 (D), A:7 (E), A:10 (G), A:12 (A)
2. Removing B and F removes every half step. With no notes one fret apart, it's very hard to hit a note that clashes.

### Practice
- Find: play E minor pentatonic on the low E string, starting open. → E:0 (E), E:3 (G), E:5 (A), E:7 (B), E:10 (D), E:12 (E)

### Quiz
Q1 (choose) The notes of A minor pentatonic are...
- A C D E G ✓
- A B C D E → fix: That's the first five notes of the minor scale. The pentatonic removes B and F instead.

Q2 (choose) Which two notes of A natural minor are removed?
- B and F ✓
- C and G → fix: C and G stay. B (2) and F (♭6) go.

Q3 (play) On the A string, play the A minor pentatonic note between E and the next A. → A:10 (G)

**Remember:** Minor pentatonic = natural minor without 2 and ♭6.

---

## 8.2 Box 1 on the guitar

Chip: Fact
**Why it matters:** This is the shape most guitarists learn first. Now you'll know which notes are in it.

### Teach
1. Box 1 of A minor pentatonic sits at frets 5 to 8. It starts on A, because A is at fret 5 on the low E string. Play it from low to high.
   - Play: E:5 (A), E:8 (C), A:5 (D), A:7 (E), D:5 (G), D:7 (A), G:5 (C), G:7 (D), B:5 (E), B:8 (G), e:5 (A), e:8 (C)
2. The home notes (A) are at three places in the box.
   - Play: E:5 (A), D:7 (A), e:5 (A)
3. The shape is movable. Start it on G (low E fret 3) and it becomes G minor pentatonic.

### Practice
- Find: play every C in the box. → E:8 (C), G:5 (C), e:8 (C)

### Quiz
Q1 (choose) Why does box 1 of A minor pentatonic start at fret 5?
- A is at fret 5 on the low E string ✓
- All boxes start at fret 5 → fix: The box starts wherever the home note is on the low E string.

Q2 (choose) Box 1 of G minor pentatonic starts at...
- Fret 3 ✓
- Fret 5 → fix: G is at fret 3 on the low E string.

Q3 (play) Play the highest C in box 1. → e:8 (C)

**Remember:** Box 1 starts on the home note on the low E string.

---

## 8.3 The major pentatonic

Chip: Fact
**Why it matters:** The same box can sound happy or sad, depending on the chord under it.

### Teach
1. The **major pentatonic** is the major scale without notes 4 and 7. C major without F and B gives **C D E G A**: the same notes as A minor pentatonic.
2. Over a C chord, C feels like home. Over Am, A does.
   - Listen: C strummed, then G:5 (C), G:7 (D), B:5 (E), B:8 (G), e:5 (A), e:8 (C)
   - Listen: Am strummed, then the same notes

### Practice
- Find: play the box note that feels like home over a C chord, on the G string. → G:5 (C)

### Quiz
Q1 (choose) Do C major pentatonic and A minor pentatonic share the same notes?
- Yes ✓
- No → fix: C D E G A and A C D E G are the same five notes.

Q2 (choose) G major pentatonic is...
- G A B D E ✓
- G A B C D → fix: Remove notes 4 (C) and 7 (F♯) from G major.

Q3 (choose) Which minor pentatonic has the same notes as G major pentatonic?
- E minor pentatonic ✓
- A minor pentatonic → fix: E is G major's relative minor.

**Remember:** Same five notes, different home, depending on the chord.

---

## 8.4 The blue note

Chip: Fact (the note). Common guideline (how to use it).
**Why it matters:** One extra note gives the pentatonic its bluesy edge.

### Teach
1. The **blue note** is ♭5: in A, it's **E♭**, between D and E. In box 1 it's at A:6 and G:8.
   - Play: A:5 (D), A:6 (E♭), A:7 (E)
2. It's very tense, so pass through it and don't stop on it. On the higher strings: D, E♭, then land on E.
   - Listen: Am strummed
   - Play: G:7 (D), G:8 (E♭), B:5 (E)

### Quiz
Q1 (choose) The blue note in E minor pentatonic is...
- B♭ ✓
- C → fix: The blue note is ♭5: 6 half steps above E is B♭.

Q2 (choose) The blue note works best as...
- A quick passing note ✓
- The last note of a phrase → fix: It's very tense. Pass through it and land on a stable note.

Q3 (play) Play the blue note on the A string in box 1. → A:6 (E♭)

**Remember:** The blue note is ♭5. Pass through it, don't stop on it.

# Module 9: Improvising

**Intro line:** "You've learned the theory. Now you'll use it, playing over a backing track."

Backing for every lesson: four bars of 4/4 at 80 bpm, **Am, F, G, Am**, looping. Notes allowed: box 1 of A minor pentatonic (8.2), plus E♭ from Lesson 9.5.

Chord notes inside box 1 (these glow during the matching bar when hints are on):

| Chord | Glowing positions |
|---|---|
| Am (A, C, E) | E:5 (A), E:8 (C), A:7 (E), D:7 (A), G:5 (C), B:5 (E), e:5 (A), e:8 (C) |
| F (F, A, C) | E:5 (A), E:8 (C), D:7 (A), G:5 (C), e:5 (A), e:8 (C) |
| G (G, B, D) | A:5 (D), D:5 (G), G:7 (D), B:8 (G) |

## 9.1 Aim for the chord

Chip: Common guideline
**Why it matters:** This is the step from running the scale to soloing: aim at the notes of the chord that's playing.

### Teach
1. As the backing plays, the glowing notes change every bar. They're the notes of the chord playing right now.
   - Listen: backing, with the glow moving bar by bar
2. Play anything you like from the box, but end each bar on a glowing note.

### Practice
- Play along for one loop. Pass: the last note in each bar is a chord note for that bar.

### Quiz
Q1 (choose) Over F, which box notes are chord notes?
- A and C ✓
- D and G → fix: F is F, A, C. In the box you have A and C.

Q2 (choose) Over G, which box notes are chord notes?
- G and D ✓
- A and C → fix: G is G, B, D. In the box you have G and D.

**Remember:** Aim for the chord, not just the scale.

---

## 9.2 Passing notes and landing

Chip: Common guideline
**Why it matters:** Phrases sound finished when they land well.

### Teach
1. Notes that aren't chord notes are fine in the middle of a phrase. They lead into chord notes.
2. Over the last Am bar, ending on A sounds finished. Ending on D sounds like it wants to keep going.
   - Listen: B:8 (G), B:5 (E), B:8 (G), G:7 (D) over Am
   - Listen: B:8 (G), B:5 (E), B:8 (G), e:5 (A) over Am

### Practice
- Play bar 4 (Am) and end on A. → accept note A as the last note of bar 4

### Quiz
Q1 (choose) Which ending sounds most finished over the last Am bar?
- A ✓
- D → fix: D isn't in Am. A is the home note.

**Remember:** Pass through tension, land on a chord note.

---

## 9.3 One idea, four bars

Chip: Common guideline
**Why it matters:** Good solos often say one idea, repeat it, change it, then finish.

### Teach
1. Play a short idea (3 or 4 notes) in bar 1. The app records it.
2. Then choose what happens next: **Repeat it**, **Change the rhythm**, or **Answer it** (turn the shape around and land on a chord note). These are the builder moves.
3. Finish bar 4 on A.
   - Listen (example): bar 1 B:5 (E), B:8 (G), e:5 (A); bar 2 the same; bar 3 e:5 (A), B:8 (G), G:7 (D); bar 4 B:8 (G), B:5 (E), B:8 (G), e:5 (A)

### Practice
- Build four bars from your own idea. Pass: two bars share the same idea, and bar 4 ends on A.

**Remember:** One idea, said again, changed, then finished.

---

## 9.4 Rhythm and space

Chip: Common guideline
**Why it matters:** Silence and off-beat notes make a phrase sound like music, not an exercise.

### Teach
1. Leave a gap. A rest lets the listener hear your idea.
2. Start one note on an "and" (off the beat).
   - Listen: B:5 (E), rest, B:8 (G), e:5 (A), starting off the beat

### Practice
- Play one loop with at least one rest inside a bar and one note starting off the beat.

**Remember:** Where a note lands matters as much as which note it is.

---

## 9.5 The blue note in a phrase

Chip: Common guideline

### Teach
1. Add E♭ (G:8 or A:6) once, passing from D to E.
   - Listen: G:7 (D), G:8 (E♭), B:5 (E) over Am

### Practice
- Play one loop using E♭ once. Pass: E♭ is followed straight away by D or E, and never ends a bar.

**Remember:** The blue note is a stepping stone.

---

## 9.6 Final challenge: four bars, no help

No glowing notes, no moves. Show the goals first:
1. Every bar ends on a chord note for that bar.
2. Bar 4 ends on A.
3. At least two bars share the same idea.
4. At least one rest inside a bar.
5. At most one scale run (three or more notes stepping through the box in one direction).

Score: goals met out of 5. Save the first try and show later tries beside it.
